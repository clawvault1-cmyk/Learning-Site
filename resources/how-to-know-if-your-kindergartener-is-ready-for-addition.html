Parent tips

Is my child ready to add?

Addition looks simple to adults. Two plus three is five. For a five-year-old, that small problem rests on several skills that have to be in place first. If a child starts adding before those skills are steady, math can feel confusing. You can check at home in a few minutes.

What kindergarten addition involves

The Common Core State Standards for kindergarten describe addition as "putting together and adding to." Kindergartners are expected to show addition using objects, fingers, drawings, and later written problems, and to add within 10. They are also expected to become fluent with addition and subtraction within 5 by the end of the year. The year starts with hands-on understanding and builds toward written problems, not the other way around.

Five signs your child may be ready

1. They count objects one at a time. Ask your child to count six crackers. Watch whether they touch or point to each one as they say a number, without skipping or double-counting.

2. They know the last number tells "how many." After counting the crackers, ask, "So how many are there?" A child who is ready answers "six" without counting again. A child who starts over is still building this idea, which is normal.

3. They recognize small groups quickly. Many children can glance at two or three dots, or the faces of a die, and know the amount without counting. That makes adding feel easier.

4. They recognize and can write numerals to 10. Handwriting does not need to be perfect, but they should be able to match the written "4" to four objects.

5. They understand "more." Put three blocks down, add one more, and ask what happened. If your child can tell you there are more now, they already understand the core idea of addition.

Quick home check: Put two toy cars on the table. Say, "Here come two more cars," and add them. Ask, "How many cars now?" Notice how your child solves it: counting all, counting on from two, or just knowing. Any of these is a good sign. Guessing at random suggests a little more counting practice first.

Signs to wait a little longer

Your child may benefit from more counting play before addition pages if they:

  • Skip numbers or lose track when counting more than five objects
  • Count the same object twice or skip objects
  • Can recite numbers but can't match a numeral to a group
  • Become upset or shut down when asked "how many"

None of these are problems. They just mean the next step is counting, not adding. Children develop at different rates. NAEYC's guidance on developmentally appropriate practice says to build on each child's strengths and meet them where they are, rather than pushing ahead on a fixed schedule.

How to build readiness through everyday play

The IES guide Teaching Math to Young Children recommends teaching number skills in a sensible order and using everyday situations and games. You can do this at home:

  • Count real things. Stairs, grapes, socks in the laundry basket.
  • Play board games with a die. Moving a token space by space practices one-to-one counting.
  • Talk about "one more." "You have four blueberries. If I give you one more, how many?"
  • Use fingers freely. Fingers are a useful tool, not a crutch, at this age.

When you're ready to start

Begin with very small sums, such as totals up to 5, and use pictures your child can count. Keep sessions short. A few problems done with focus are better than a full page done in tears. If the first page goes smoothly, try another. If it's a struggle, step back to counting for a week or two and try again.

The most important thing to remember: being ready for addition is not about age, or about what a neighbor's child is doing. It is about whether the building blocks are in place.
