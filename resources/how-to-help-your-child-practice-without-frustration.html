How to Keep Practice From Turning Into a Fight

Many parents know the scene: you sit down with a worksheet, and ten minutes later someone is in tears. Sometimes it's the child. Sometimes it's you. At-home practice doesn't have to look like that. A few small changes in timing, setup, and the way you respond can make a big difference.

1. Check the level first

The most common cause of frustration is work that is simply too hard. If your child is guessing or needing help on most problems, the issue probably isn't attitude. Step back to an easier page. NAEYC's guidance on developmentally appropriate practice emphasizes building on what each child can already do, and that applies to home practice too.

2. Keep sessions short

Young children have limited attention spans, and they vary a lot from child to child and day to day. For many 4- to 6-year-olds, a focused 5 to 10 minutes is plenty. Stopping while your child is still engaged makes them more willing to come back next time. You don't have to finish the page.

3. Pick a good time

Avoid practicing when your child is hungry, tired, or just got home from a long day. Many families find a calm moment after a snack or on a weekend morning works best. A predictable routine, like "one page after breakfast on Saturdays," can reduce arguments because it's simply what you do.

4. Set up for success

  • Clear the table of distractions and turn off screens.
  • Have a good pencil, a crayon, or a marker ready. Some children love using a special pen.
  • Keep helpful tools nearby: blocks, buttons, or cereal for counting.
  • Sit beside your child rather than across from them.

5. Use hands-on tools freely

Fingers, counters, and drawings aren't cheating. The Common Core kindergarten math standards specifically describe children representing addition with objects, fingers, and drawings. The What Works Clearinghouse practice guide Teaching Math to Young Children also recommends helping children use math language and connecting math to everyday situations. Letting your child touch, move, and talk through problems often turns confusion into understanding.

6. Praise effort and strategy, not just answers

Instead of "You're so smart," try specific comments about what your child did:

  • "You used your fingers to check. Good thinking."
  • "You kept trying even when that one was tricky."
  • "I noticed you counted on from 4 instead of starting over."

This kind of feedback focuses on things your child can control.

7. Handle mistakes calmly

Mistakes are information. Instead of "No, that's wrong," try:

  • "Let's check that one together."
  • "Can you show me with the blocks?"
  • "Count them with me one more time."

Often children catch their own mistake when asked to explain. If the same mistake keeps happening, it's a sign to revisit an earlier step, not to drill harder.

When tears start: Stop. Say something like, "This one is tough. Let's take a break." Do something fun, and come back another day, perhaps with an easier page. Pushing through a meltdown rarely leads to learning and can make the next session harder.

8. Mix in play

Worksheets are one tool among many. Board games, counting snacks, or counting stairs on a walk all build the same skills. Alternating a worksheet with a quick game keeps practice light. You might turn a page into a game, too: roll a die to choose which problem to do next, or give a sticker for each row.

9. Let your child have some choices

Small choices give children a sense of control: Which page first? Pencil or crayon? Sit at the table or on the floor? Do the top half now and the bottom half after a snack?

10. Watch your own stress

Children pick up on adult frustration quickly. If you notice yourself getting tense, it's okay to end the session. Remember that one hard day doesn't define your child's progress. Consistent, relaxed practice over weeks matters far more than any single page.

The bottom line

The goal of practice at this age isn't just correct answers. It's helping your child feel that learning is something they can do. Short sessions, the right level, hands-on tools, and calm encouragement go a long way toward making that happen.
