Parent tips

How to keep practice from turning into a fight

Many parents know this scene: you sit down with a worksheet, and ten minutes later someone is in tears. Sometimes it is the child. Sometimes it is you. A few small changes can help.

1. Check the level first

The most common cause of frustration is work that is simply too hard. If your child is guessing or needs help on most problems, the issue is probably not attitude. Step back to an easier page. NAEYC's guidance on developmentally appropriate practice says to build on what each child can already do. That applies at home too.

2. Keep sessions short

Young children have short attention spans, and they vary from day to day. For many 4- to 6-year-olds, 5 to 10 focused minutes is plenty. Stop while your child is still willing. You do not have to finish the page.

3. Pick a good time

Avoid practice when your child is hungry, tired, or just got home from a long day. A calm moment after a snack, or a weekend morning, often works better. A simple routine, like "one page after breakfast on Saturdays," can reduce arguments.

4. Set up for success

  • Clear the table and turn off screens.
  • Have a pencil or crayon ready. Some children like a special pen.
  • Keep counters nearby, such as blocks, buttons, or cereal.
  • Sit beside your child, not across from them.

5. Use hands-on tools freely

Fingers, counters, and drawings are not cheating. The Common Core kindergarten math standards describe children showing addition with objects, fingers, and drawings. The IES guide Teaching Math to Young Children also recommends helping children use math words and connecting math to everyday situations. Letting your child touch, move, and talk through problems often turns confusion into understanding.

6. Praise effort and strategy, not just answers

Instead of "You're so smart," try comments about what your child did:

  • "You used your fingers to check. Good thinking."
  • "You kept trying even when that one was tricky."
  • "I noticed you counted on from 4 instead of starting over."

This kind of feedback focuses on things your child can control.

7. Handle mistakes calmly

Mistakes are information. Instead of "No, that's wrong," try:

  • "Let's check that one together."
  • "Can you show me with the blocks?"
  • "Count them with me one more time."

Children often catch their own mistake when asked to explain. If the same mistake keeps happening, go back to an earlier step. Don't drill harder.

When tears start: Stop. Say something like, "This one is tough. Let's take a break." Do something fun, and come back another day, maybe with an easier page. Pushing through a meltdown rarely helps learning.

8. Mix in play

Worksheets are one tool among many. Board games, counting snacks, or counting stairs all build the same skills. You might turn a page into a game too: roll a die to choose the next problem, or give a sticker for each row.

9. Let your child have some choices

Small choices give children a sense of control: Which page first? Pencil or crayon? Sit at the table or on the floor?

10. Watch your own stress

Children pick up on adult frustration quickly. If you notice yourself getting tense, it is okay to end the session. One hard day does not define your child's progress. Calm practice over weeks matters more than any single page.

The bottom line

The goal of practice at this age is not just correct answers. It is helping your child feel that learning is something they can do. Short sessions, the right level, hands-on tools, and calm encouragement go a long way.
