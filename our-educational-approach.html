Our educational approach

Built on public frameworks, explained in plain language

We organize every worksheet around one skill, one level, and a clear learning goal. To decide what those skills and levels should be, we draw on recognized, publicly available education frameworks.

Please note: We are not affiliated with, endorsed by, approved by, or certified by any of the organizations below. Our worksheets are informed by their published work. We don't claim our worksheets are scientifically proven to improve academic outcomes.

Common Core State Standards

A set of K–12 expectations for what students should learn in math and English Language Arts at each grade. For Kindergarten math, the standards describe adding and subtracting within 10 using objects, drawings, and equations.

How we use it: We use them to sequence K–2 skills and list relevant codes (for example, K.OA.A.1–K.OA.A.5) only where we've checked the standard text. See Kindergarten Operations & Algebraic Thinking and Kindergarten Reading: Foundational Skills.

Official source ↗

Head Start Early Learning Outcomes Framework (ELOF)

A federal framework describing skills children typically develop from birth to age 5, including early math and literacy.

How we use it: We use it to shape Pre-K worksheets so they fit what young children are developmentally ready for.

Official source ↗

NAEYC: Developmentally Appropriate Practice

The National Association for the Education of Young Children describes developmentally appropriate practice: teaching that meets children where they are and supports each next step.

How we use it: We keep pages short and uncluttered, and we build each level on the last one.

Official source ↗

What Works Clearinghouse (U.S. Department of Education, IES)

Reviews education research and publishes practice guides, including Teaching Math to Young Children and Foundational Skills to Support Reading for Understanding in K–3.

How we use it: We use the guides' recommendations, such as developmental progressions in early math, to organize our levels.

Official source ↗

Structured Literacy & the Science of Reading

Structured literacy teaches reading explicitly and in order: sounds, letters, decoding, spelling, then fluency. It's based on research into how children learn to read.

How we use it: Our upcoming reading worksheets follow a planned phonics sequence instead of mixing skills at random.

Official source ↗
