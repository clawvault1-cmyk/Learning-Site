Our educational approach

Where the practice ideas come from

We look at learning standards and research-informed teaching guides. Then we turn them into short pages you can print. The guides shape the order of practice. They do not score your child.

A clear line: We are not affiliated with, endorsed by, or certified by any school, district, state, or the organizations below. These worksheets are not scientifically proven. No school partnership is claimed. “Informed by” means we read the public sources and used them to choose skills and sequence.

What that means at the kitchen table

  • Start from a skill your child can already do.
  • Teach the next idea with objects, then a picture, then symbols.
  • Keep each pack on one skill so the next page is not a surprise.

Foundations we use

The left column names the source. The right column says how it shapes the materials.

FoundationHow it informs the materials
Common Core and Florida B.E.S.T.These are learning standards. They describe what kindergarten math includes. Both treat addition as putting groups together, with objects, drawings, and number sentences, and with totals up to 10. Common Core also asks children to break numbers into parts, find what makes 10, and add within 5 with ease. Florida’s B.E.S.T. benchmarks ask for those same kinds of steps, including short real-world problems an adult can read aloud. Our kindergarten addition packs stay inside those ideas. We name the standards we read. We do not claim a state or a school approved the pages.

Institute of Education Sciences / What Works ClearinghouseThe U.S. Department of Education reviews research and publishes practice guides. Teaching Math to Young Children recommends teaching number ideas in a planned order and building on what a child already knows. Foundational Skills to Support Reading for Understanding in Kindergarten Through 3rd Grade recommends linking sounds in speech to letters, teaching children to blend those sounds, and practicing the spelling patterns they have been taught, including common words. We use that order for levels. Reading packs are not for sale yet. When they are, they will follow this sound-to-letter path.

NAEYC developmentally appropriate practiceThe National Association for the Education of Young Children describes teaching that fits the child in front of you. It builds on what that child can already do, keeps learning engaging, and fits the child’s age, strengths, and background. It is not a race through a checklist. We keep pages short, say what to try first, and tell you when to stay with a skill.

Structured literacyThe International Dyslexia Association describes structured literacy as teaching sounds, letters, and spelling patterns directly, in order, from easier patterns to harder ones. Children are not left to guess the pattern from a mixed pile of words. Future reading pages will teach one pattern, then offer practice with that pattern. This is an approach we are using. It is not a claim that these pages treat dyslexia, and no clinic has reviewed them.

Head Start Early Learning Outcomes FrameworkThis federal framework describes how children grow from birth to age 5, including early math and language. Head Start says the framework is not a test, and it should not be used to decide that a child has failed or is not ready for kindergarten. Pre-K pages, when they are ready, will stay in that early range: counting, sounds, and letters, in small steps. We will not use the framework as a scorecard.

How reading practice will work

Reading packs are not for sale yet. Here is the plan, in everyday words. Kindergarten reading standards for foundational skills are published with the Common Core kindergarten reading standards.

Sounds and letters go together

A child learns that a letter stands for a sound. The letter m stands for the sound at the beginning of map. Seeing the letter and saying the sound are practiced together, not as two unrelated lists.

Blend and spell

Blending means pushing sounds together to read a word. Spelling means pulling a word apart and writing the letters. Children practice the pattern they were just taught. They are not handed a mix of patterns on the same page.

CVC words, in plain English

CVC words are simple words like cat, map, and sit. You can hear three sounds: a consonant, a short vowel, and another consonant. In cat, you can hear the sounds at the start, in the middle, and at the end. There is no silent letter in these starter words. A longer explanation is in our parent guide to CVC words.

Sight words

A sight word is a word children see often, such as the, said, or was. Some of these words follow a pattern the child has learned. Others have a part that does not. Practice will still connect the sounds and the spelling, and it will point out the irregular part. A sight word is not only a card to memorize with no link to the letters.
